I read this (for at least the fourth time since January 2023) a year and a half ago and confessed to feeling some story fatigue then, so I felt nervous about revisiting it again so relatively soon. I needed an audiobook listen, though, and I've been doing some stuff at church that has me thinking about "Walkaway church" and how genuinely inspirational I find this book, so I went ahead and started listening again. It was amazing. I love Doctorow's voice and tone in this book, I adore the worldbuilding and throwaway references that raise really interesting questions, and the story just makes me so dang happy. I have often joked to myself (and again during this relisten) that I would be happy to add it to my personal canon—not just a literary canon but a scriptural one.

It is also interesting to reread it after having consumed so much Jacques Ellul recently. Ellul and Doctorow differ from each other in some very interesting ways. Ellul is fundamentally pessimistic about *technique* but ultimately concerned with *the technical phenomenon*—the social and personal attitudes that we bring to technology. Doctorow is fundamentally optimistic about technology in a way that I've struggled with as I've grown more pessimistic myself, especially about (generative) AI. Yet, even as I'm increasingly skeptical about "technology as solution," books like this one remind me that Doctorow is just as critical of the technical phenomenon as Ellul is. When Ellul concedes that we could learn to use individual technologies appropriately if we could ever learn to divorce ourselves from the attitude of ever needing to do more, do better, be richer, etc., I think of Doctorow's walkaways, who give a (fictional) example of how that might be possible.

Funnily enough, in the context of this last paragraph, it was thanks to this book that I started reading Ellul. There are clear anarchist influences in *Walkaway*, and when that finally registered with me, I started reading up on anarchism more broadly, to see if there were other writings in this vein that I found as inspirational and motivating as this one. I can draw a straight line, for example, between what I find inspiring in David Graeber's hopefulness in humanity and the mental fist pumps I do whenever I get to the climax of this book. If Doctorow led me to anarchism, it was discovering that Christian anarchism was a thing that eventually led me to Ellul, so this book has affected my career (and broader life) in a big, big way.

I am hard-pressed to think of a book I love more than this one, and I loved relistening to it over the past couple of weeks. I want to keep drawing inspiration from it, including as I do work in the church. More frivolously, I really want to reskin the excellent anarchist solo RPG *Why We Fight* as a Walkaway game, so keep an eye out for that kind of nonsense.
