When I'm reviewing a scholarly book on this blog, I'm doing it drom the perspective of how important its argument is and how useful it is for my research. This book's argument is important, and I think I'll find it useful as I move forward with thinking about the Latter-day Saint technical system.

I once read (or heard or watched—I can't track it down just now) something from a scholar of the Bible who suggested that it was an oversimplification to suggest that similarities between the creation account in Genesis and other ancient religions' equivalent accounts meant that the authors of the former had stolen ideas from the latter. Rather, they argued, it was more accurate to think of all of these religions being formed in similar historical and cultural circumstances, which would necessarily lead to parallels. This book is important for the way it invites us to think about the Book of Mormon in similar terms. This isn't to downplay Tom's criticism of an appropriation of Indigenous ideas and traditions in the composing of the Book of Mormon, which I think is another important contribution. Rather, it's to say that even if the "causal mechanisms" behind similarities are murkier than Tom sometimes suggests (and I think they are necessarily murky), it is productive to consider Tom's deeper argument: that the Book of Mormon fits into a broader tradition of challenging the Christian canon with Indigenous understandings.

This last bit also hints at why this book will be personally useful to me. Davids Graeber and Wengrow frame narratives of technological progress as responses to Indigenous critiques of European political perspectives, and as I think about Mormonism as a technical phenomenon, the Book of Mormon clearly plays into that dynamic. Tom helpfully points out the way that the Book of Mormon echoes another Ellulian dynamic in a similar way: It asserts the supremacy of the written word in response to Indigenous pushback against it. This is an exciting discovery for me as I think about Mormonism in Ellulian terms.

So, yes, I do think the picture is fuzzier than Tom sometimes makes things out to be, and I think the book could be stronger if streamlined and trimmed in some parts. What a contribution, though! I'm grateful to have read it.
